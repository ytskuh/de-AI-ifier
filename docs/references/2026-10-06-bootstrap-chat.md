

## Rhetorical habits: count grammar, not words

The first-hand source here is Reinhart et al., *PNAS* 2025 (122(8):e2422455122, "Do LLMs write like humans?"). They operationalized "rhetorical habits" using Douglas Biber's 66/67 grammatical features and found that instruction-tuned models strongly prefer present participial clauses, "that" clauses as subjects, nominalization, and phrasal coordination — with GPT-4o using present participial clauses at 5.3x the human rate ("Bryan, leaning on his agility, dances around the ring, evading..."). Nominalizations run 1.5–2x human rates, while GPT-4o uses agentless passives at roughly half the human rate. Two practically important findings: model families have distinct "house styles" — GPT-4o avoids clausal coordination while Llama 3 overuses it, and they diverge on downtoners like "barely" or "nearly" — so calibrate against the model you actually use. And it's all machine-countable: a random forest on just these features misclassified only 4.2% of LLM texts as human.

The tooling is open. The features are extracted with the pseudobibeR package as occurrence rates per 1,000 words, and their human–AI parallel corpus and pre-extracted features are on Hugging Face. So your pipeline becomes: run pseudobibeR (or a spaCy reimplementation) over your pre-LLM baseline corpus and the candidate article, compute per-1,000-word rates, and flag any feature outside, say, the baseline's 5th–95th percentile. Each flag maps to a mechanical rewrite rule: participial clause → finite clause or sentence split; nominalization → restore the verb; "that"-clause subject → restructure.

For habits Biber's set doesn't cover, write template detectors over a dependency parse: triadic coordination chains (the rule of three), "not just X, but Y" frames, sentence-final participial tails, em-dash and colon density, and document-level uniformity metrics (coefficient of variation of sentence lengths and paragraph lengths — humans are lumpier). A nice upgrade: generate LLM articles on the same topics as your baseline corpus, train a logistic regression on all these features, and read off the coefficients — that gives you a personalized, ranked tell-list instead of a generic one.

## Statistical signature: measure it with a language model

This layer is defined in terms of token probabilities, so the meter has to be an LLM. The practical zero-shot options, both open-source:

**Binoculars** (Hans et al., ICML 2024, arXiv:2401.12070) is the strongest simple option. It scores text with an observer/performer model pair, taking the ratio of ordinary perplexity to cross-perplexity (how well the observer agrees with the performer's next-token predictions) — this matters because raw perplexity alone is unreliable; unusual topics inflate it arbitrarily, and a GPT-4 capybara-astrophysicist paragraph fooled both GPTZero and DetectGPT. It detects over 90% of generated samples at a 0.01% false positive rate with no training data, and the reference implementation is at github.com/ahans30/Binoculars. **Fast-DetectGPT** (Bao et al., arXiv:2310.05130) is the cheap alternative — it uses conditional probability curvature, sampling alternative word choices and comparing their probabilities. Either gives you sentence-level scores, which is what you need to *localize* the most machine-like sentences rather than just get a document verdict. Burstiness you can compute directly alongside: variance of per-sentence perplexity plus sentence-length CV.

Here's the catch with your remediation plan, though: "ask the LLM to regenerate the sentence avoiding those words" fixes the lexical layer but barely touches this one, because the regenerated sentence is still a maximum-likelihood sequence — low perplexity by construction. The signature is a property of *how the tokens were chosen*, not which words. What actually moves it: human token-level edits (every concrete fact, number, or idiosyncratic phrasing you insert is a low-probability token the model wouldn't have picked), cross-family paraphrasing, higher-temperature sampling, and — most fundamentally — avoiding the chat-tuned distribution, since base models without post-training already read as human to detectors and Reinhart found the stylistic divergence is larger for instruction-tuned models than base models. Also budget for the arms race: GPTZero now benchmarks specifically against output from 12+ paraphrasing tools, and even when automatic detectors are beaten, expert human readers remained accurate against text explicitly humanized using expert-derived instructions — the residual tell is content-level genericity, which only real specifics fix.

Is there opensource tools already implemented/utilized these with high quality (as a useable tool to help polish up articles)?

Short answer after digging through GitHub, arXiv, and the tooling ecosystem: **there is no single mature, end-to-end open-source "article de-AI-ifier."** What exists is (a) high-quality *measurement* components, (b) a fast-growing crop of small detect-and-rewrite tools (mostly LLM agent skills, young and unevenly curated), and (c) evasion-focused paraphrasers you should avoid for quality work. Here's the map, by layer:

## Statistical signature (the strongest components)

- **Binoculars** (github.com/ahans30/Binoculars, ICML 2024) — the reference implementation of the observer/performer perplexity-ratio method; free, open-source, no training required, with strong reported performance across domains including short texts. Peer-reviewed, and the de facto standard zero-shot meter.
- **Fast-DetectGPT** (github.com/baoguangsheng/fast-detect-gpt) — the conditional-probability-curvature method, sampling alternative word choices and scoring them with an LM; much cheaper per document.
- For an actual *polishing workflow* rather than a research script, there's a newer project listed first under github.com/topics/humanization (Python) that is the closest thing to what you described: a local, full-logit forensics and humanization toolkit that scores markdown with observer/performer llama.cpp models (logPPL, logXPPL, Binoculars score), surfaces chunk-aware hotspots and heatmaps, ranks rewrites, and ships a CLI, desktop UI, Obsidian plugin, VS Code extension, and HTTP API. That's exactly the "localize the AI-est sentences while editing" loop — but it's a young, dev-grade project, not battle-tested infrastructure.

## Lexical layer (most mature ecosystem)

- **slop-forensics** (github.com/sam-paech/slop-forensics) — builds "slop profiles" per model: over-represented words, bigrams, and trigrams versus human baselines, filtered against naturally frequent English words, plus a combined slop index. This automates your pre-LLM-baseline comparison plan, including phrase-level n-grams.
- **antislop-sampler / antislop-vllm / auto-antislop** (same author) — fixes the problem at generation time: it down-weights tokens leading into banned words/phrases during inference, and its regex_bans feature backtracks and resamples to block structural patterns like "not x, but y". auto-antislop chains this into a full pipeline that accumulates ban lists and produces preference pairs for FTPO fine-tuning. There's a formal paper behind it now (arXiv:2510.15061) which found some slop patterns appear over 1,000× more frequently in LLM output than human text. The author is candid that it's research-grade code and recommends rolling your own slop list — which you're already planning to do.
- **Vale** (github.com/errata-ai/vale) as delivery vehicle — a fast, markup-aware, highly customizable prose linter with VS Code/CI integration. I found no polished public "AI-slop" Vale style package yet, but converting Kobak's excess_words.csv or a slop-forensics profile into Vale existence/regex rules is an afternoon's work. PostHog documents exactly the architecture you want: "never send an LLM to do a linter's job" — use Vale to detect issues deterministically, then use LLMs to fix them.

## Structural/rhetorical layer

- **pybiber** (github.com/browndw/pybiber) — extracts the 67 Biber lexicogrammatical features (tense/aspect, subordination, nominalizations, modals, etc.) on spaCy + Polars, with full multi-dimensional analysis. Same author as pseudobibeR, which is what the PNAS study itself used, counting features per 1,000 words from dependency-parsed text — so you'd be measuring with the literature's own instrument. Note pybiber gives you *numbers*, not edits; you supply the baseline comparison and rewrite rules.
- **BiberPlus / Neurobiber** (arXiv:2502.18590) — a Python toolkit tracking 96 features (Biber's 67 plus newer ones), rule-based over spaCy parses with 225 unit tests, alongside a fast neural tagger. biberpy is another option with French, Russian, and Spanish ports if you work multilingually.

## Integrated detect-and-rewrite tools (useful, but young)

- **humanizer-skill** (github.com/Aboudjem/humanizer-skill) — 43 numbered patterns (~70% derived from Wikipedia's editor-curated "Signs of AI writing" list), 5 voice profiles, detect/rewrite/edit modes with a 0–100 AI-tell score, in one Markdown file with zero API calls. The Wikipedia grounding makes its pattern list more defensible than most.
- **no_ai_slop_writing_rules** (realrossmanngroup) — interesting as a template: a hard anti-slop rule set plus a data-driven voice profile built from corpus analysis of 513,683 words of one author's writing — sentence-length variance, testable-number density, claim-then-proof paragraph structure. That second half is the blueprint for turning *your* pre-LLM corpus into a target profile, not just a ban list.
- **NousResearch's ANTI-SLOP.md** (in the autonovel repo) — a well-curated rules document; it identifies "It's not just X — it's Y" as the single most overused pattern across models' top trigram lists per slop-forensics.
- Smaller CLI options exist (e.g., ai-that-works/deslop, a CLI that flags generic phrasing, repetitive structure, vague claims, and unnatural transitions, then rewrites), plus language-specific skills — one covers Korean, English, Chinese, and Japanese patterns and runs as a Claude Code/Cursor skill or standalone Node CLI.

## What I'd skip

The "bypass Turnitin/GPTZero" repos — e.g., lynote-ai/humanize-text, which round-trips text through translation chains (Chinese → Japanese → Finnish) plus a temperature-1.3 DeepSeek rewrite to break statistical fingerprints. These optimize the detector score, not the prose, and given that detectors now train on humanizer output and human experts see through it anyway, they're the wrong tool for polishing articles.

## The practical stack

For your use case, quality lives in the components, not in any packaged product: **slop-forensics or your own excess-vocab analysis → Vale rules** (lexical), **pybiber rates vs. your baseline percentiles** (structural), **Binoculars/Fast-DetectGPT sentence scores** (statistical), feeding flagged spans into a constrained LLM rewrite — with the regex-class patterns ("not just X, but Y", triads, participial tails) handled either by Vale or, if you control generation, by antislop's regex bans so they never appear.
